A while back there were a couple of opinion articles flying around about whether to keep the SAT as a means for selecting students for elite universities. In particular, there was an NYT opinions column by Ross Douthat that got me a little worked up. Without going into too much detail, the article argued that the SAT is essential to identify applicants who merit admission to ivy league-type schools. 

I wholeheartedly agree that the SAT serves a very useful metric in measuring education and critical thinking skills, but think that those who argue over whether we should keep, make optional, or completely eliminate the SAT as a part of the application process for US universities are basing their arguments off an unreasonable assumption. 

Here is my letter in response to Ross [as published in the NYT LTTE](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/05/13/opinion/letters/sat-merit-college-admissions.html):

> Ross Douthat’s concept of meritocracy misses the forest for the trees. The number of spots at Ivy League-type schools is artificially limited; there will always be meritorious students outside the elite bubble. Merit is determined by putting your education to good use after finishing college, not by the arbitrary number your school was assigned by some rankings conglomerate.
>
>I attended a respectable but not “elite” school in Ohio for my bachelor’s degree. I still got an amazing education and was surrounded by incredible classmates. My experience in industry and graduate school is that people from nonelite schools frequently compete with or outperform peers from pedigreed schools. Rankings simply cannot accurately describe the quality and applicability of the instruction students receive in college.
>
>So let’s agree as a society to drop the obsession with college rankings and admission to brand-name schools. Instead, we should focus our collective attention on increasing funding for universities across the board. Invest in everyone’s education. This is the path to a true meritocracy.
 
Universities should pride themselves on how many people they can admit and how much of an impact they can make on the education of the students that they do admit. The obsession with exclusivity is approaching education like it's a luxury good and I think any adult who believes in a just society would reject the proposal that education should be valued like Birkin bags. And yet, that's exactly what we do in the USA with the obsession with ivy league universities. 

Public universities on the other hand are extremely efficient at turning tax payer dollars into educational opportunities for people from all sorts of backgrounds. Not to mention that they provide just as important of a home for scientific researchers as those at private institutions. Globally, America is an outlier. The top of our university rankings are occupied by the likes of Harvard, MIT, Stanford, and Yale. And somehow, we as a country have become proud of the fact that our private universities sap resources from public ones and instill an elitist educational network. Other countries just have public institutions that work. 

Our pride in our universities (and from a policy standpoint, the money we give to universities through government backed tuition loans and grants from agencies like the NSF and NIH) should be tied to how good of a job these institutions do at educating as many people as possible and facilitating as much rigorous research. When looking at this dollar for dollar, the ivy league universities are a bad investment for our society and we should treat them as such.
